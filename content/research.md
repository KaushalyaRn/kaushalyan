Hoverflies (Syrphidae) are pollinators, wanderers, and unusually good at standing still. Many species look like bees or wasps; they are flies, with a gift for hovering and a life that moves between flowers, aphid colonies, decaying matter, and the weather.

I joined the Insect Symbiosis Ecology and Evolution (ISEE) group at the University of Helsinki in December 2025 as a grant-funded researcher. My project looks at **host-associated microbiota** of Finnish hoverflies: which microbes they carry, how those communities are assembled, and whether they help the insect resist environmental stress.

## The questions

**Host species.** Hoverfly lineages differ in ecology and evolutionary history. If the microbiome tracks the host family tree, that is one kind of story. If it tracks habitat and behaviour instead, that is another.

**Diet.** Some hoverflies take pollen and nectar as adults; larvae may hunt aphids, live in rot holes, or feed in quite different places. Diet is a filter. I want to know how strongly it writes the microbial community.

**Pesticides and other stressors.** Agricultural landscapes are not empty of chemistry. I am asking whether microbial partners change after exposure, and whether any of those changes look like protection, cost, or both.

## How I got here

Before hoverflies I worked on a host–parasite relationship in Finnish rivers: glochidia of the endangered freshwater pearl mussel on Atlantic salmon, including a direct pipette infection method and measurements of larval growth and cyst form. Further back, I studied plant extracts and fish pathogens, taught entomology and ecology, and spent years in a secondary-school biology classroom.

The through-line is practical. Who lives with whom, under what conditions, and what that means when the environment is not kind.
